# recipe-note
A word square is not a picture of letters - it is a promise that sixteen letters obey two laws at once. Every row must be an ordinary English word, and the grid must equal its own transpose: column 1 spells row 1's word, column 2 spells row 2's, and so on, so the letter at row r, column c is the same letter at row c, column r. That second law is what makes the form bite. A letter is never one word's property - it sits at a crossing, shared by a row word and a column word, so changing one letter rewrites two words at once, and a grid can be full of real words and still not be a word square at all. Image models paint letter grids that look exactly like puzzles and satisfy almost none of this: a row that spells a word nobody knows, a column that quietly disagrees with its row. This flow makes the two laws the contract, and then it does something about a break. Give it a poster setting and a GLM director picks one square from a short menu printed in its own prompt (BALL SQUARE, EAST SQUARE, FISH SQUARE, MIST SQUARE) - every square verified in code, letter by letter, to obey the transpose law before it entered the menu - and copies it onto a WORD SQUARE CARD. The card carries the square's name and its four row clues, plain-English definitions, and nothing else: there are no letters on it for anyone to copy.

That is the second trick, borrowed from sudoku-check and turned to a different kind of reasoning. In most checks in this catalog the card fully describes the asset to be painted; here the asset does not exist until a second model reconstructs it. A GLM solver takes the four clues alone - "a round object that is thrown, kicked or hit in games", "a polite word for a woman" - and must retrieve the words from meaning, then prove them against the transpose law, using each crossing letter to convict a wrong guess in a different row. Only then does a third GLM turn the solved grid into a painter prompt (thin cell lines, a thick outer border, one large bold capital centred in each cell, the sixteen letters named row by row). Muse paints the poster. A blind Gemini reader, who never saw the card or the solution, transcribes the painted grid cell by cell. And the GLM auditor needs no answer key either: it scores the transcription against the clues and the law alone - CLUES (each read row a real word fitting its clue), WORDS (each read row a real word at all), TRANSPOSE (each read column spelling its row exactly, symmetry breaks named by cell pair), COMPLETE - because the clues were written to fit exactly one common-word square, so any complete grid that fits all four and breaks no symmetry is provably the carded square. A Decide judge headlines round 1: square holds, transpose broken, clues missed, cells blank, or not a word square.

That verdict is not where this flow stops - the loop closes, per the standing rule that a created asset ends in the corrected asset, not in text. A GLM editor reads the card, the blind read and the audit, re-solves the square itself from the clues and the law, and writes one concrete edit instruction: the full target grid, plus each broken cell named as R<row>C<column> with the letter it must show and the letter it shows now. The editor is gated in content, a rule this catalog learned the hard way from Molecule Check, whose flawless round-1 model was recoloured by a repair it never needed: on a perfect audit the editor must demand a verbatim unchanged copy of the poster. Muse Edit repairs the broken cells, a blind re-read transcribes the corrected poster, and the same auditor re-scores it against the same card and the same law, so the fix is itself audited and the reader can see exactly what the edit bought - or cost. The corrected poster is the flow's deliverable; the Decide verdict stays, demoted to a side output on round 1.

Why this axis is new: the catalog's nearest neighbour is sudoku-check, where a card likewise withholds the solution and a solver computes the asset before it is painted. But sudoku's law is numeric and eliminative - each digit once per row, column and box, derived from given digits - and a sudoku solution transposed is simply a different grid. The word square's law is lexical, semantic and geometric at once: the solver's raw material is meaning (a definition must be turned back into the one word it describes), and the grid must be symmetric under an operation sudoku never imposes. Its characteristic failure is therefore one no earlier check can even state: a solver retrieves a perfectly good English word for a clue, the painter paints it faithfully, every row reads as a word - and the transpose law convicts the grid at a crossing cell two rows away. Hidden-word is the other neighbour, and the contrast is just as plain: there, letters hide as the first letters of pictured objects, a one-way acrostic with no grid, no law and no solver. Here the letters are the picture, the law is the picture's whole claim, and the reasoning that produces the grid is itself on trial.

Honest limits, in the open: the auditor judges the reader's transcription, not the pixels - a letter the painter drew ambiguously (an I that reads as a T, a worn E read as F) is scored as the reader honestly read it, and a transcription slip convicts a correct poster or acquits a broken one; the two blind reads are independent, which is the only guard. Clue-fit is judged by a language model, not a dictionary oracle: the clues were written to be near-unique (each menu square was also checked to fit its own four clues and no other menu square's grid), but a solver or auditor with a different vocabulary could in principle settle on an unexpected word - the transpose score is the hard check that keeps such a settlement honest, since a wrong word almost never survives its crossings. Uniqueness is a property of the menu, not of English: the four squares and their clues were verified before being carded, so a director who copied a clue wrong would break the proof, not just the puzzle - which is why the auditor scores the read against the card as copied, and a corrupted card shows up as clues no square can fit. The editor's repair faces the known edit-model limit: asked to change one cell, Muse Edit repaints the whole poster, and a letter that was right can drift while the broken one is fixed - the re-audit exists to catch exactly that trade, and it is reported as read. And the flow audits a 4x4 square, deliberately: sixteen large capitals are what a painter can draw legibly and a reader can transcribe cell by cell; the classic 5x5 and 6x6 squares are left to the puzzle books.
