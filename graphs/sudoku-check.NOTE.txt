# recipe-note
A Sudoku is not a picture of digits - it is a promise that sixteen digits obey one law everywhere at once: every row, every column and every 2x2 box holds 1, 2, 3 and 4 exactly once. That law is global in a way a picture's other promises are not. Move one object in a Venn diagram and one score moves; change one digit in a Sudoku and up to three units break at the same time - its row, its column and its box - while fifteen innocent cells keep telling the truth. Image models paint grids that look exactly like Sudoku and satisfy almost none of this: a 3 repeated down a column, a box quietly holding two 1s and no 4. This flow makes the law the contract, and then it does something about a break. Give it a poster setting and a GLM director picks one puzzle from a short menu printed in its own prompt (CORNERS, BOOKENDS, CROSSROADS, STAIRCASE) - every puzzle a 4x4 grid with exactly 7 given digits, each one verified before it entered the menu to have exactly one completion - and copies it onto a SUDOKU CARD. The card carries the givens and nothing else: there is no answer on it for anyone to copy.

That is the second trick. In every earlier check in this catalog the card fully describes the asset to be painted; here the asset does not exist until a second model computes it. A GLM solver takes the givens alone and must derive the unique completion by logic - each empty cell's digit forced by what its row, column and box already hold - and only then does a third GLM turn the solved grid into a painter prompt (thin cell lines, thick box borders, one large bold digit centred in each cell, the sixteen digits named row by row). Muse paints the poster. A blind Gemini reader, who never saw the card or the solution, transcribes the painted grid cell by cell. And the GLM auditor needs no answer key either: it scores the transcription against the card and the law alone - GIVENS kept in their cells, ROWS all-different, COLUMNS all-different, BOXES all-different, COMPLETE - because the puzzle's solution is unique, so any complete grid that keeps the givens and breaks no unit is provably the solution. A Decide judge headlines round 1: solution holds, units broken, givens moved, cells blank, or not a sudoku.

That verdict is not where this flow stops - the loop closes, per the standing rule that a created asset ends in the corrected asset, not in text. A GLM editor reads the card, the blind read and the audit, solves the puzzle itself from the givens, and writes one concrete edit instruction: the full target grid, plus each broken cell named as R<row>C<column> with the digit it must show and the digit it shows now. The editor is gated in content, a rule this catalog learned the hard way from Molecule Check, whose flawless round-1 model was recoloured by a repair it never needed: on a perfect audit the editor must demand a verbatim unchanged copy of the poster. Muse Edit repairs the broken cells, a blind re-read transcribes the corrected poster, and the same auditor re-scores it against the same card and the same law, so the fix is itself audited and the reader can see exactly what the edit bought - or cost. The corrected poster is the flow's deliverable; the Decide verdict stays, demoted to a side output on round 1.

Why this axis is new: the catalog's nearest neighbours are venn-check, where correctness is likewise derived by a law and recomputed by the auditor, and maze-solve-audit, where a plan's validity is checked mechanically. Venn's law is per-object and independent - each icon's region follows from its own two attributes, and no icon can break another's score. Sudoku's law is relational: the same sixteen cells are partitioned three different ways at once, every cell answers to three units simultaneously, and a single wrong digit is a triple failure that no per-object audit can even state. And where the maze's architect invents both the maze and its route, this flow's card deliberately withholds the answer - the solver's completion is a computed result, not a copied spec, so the chain can fail at a place no earlier check can: the reasoning itself, before a single pixel is painted. The givens add the third difference: seven anchor cells that solver, painter and editor may complete around but never move, and whose immutability is itself scored.

Honest limits, in the open: the auditor judges the reader's transcription, not the pixels - a digit the painter drew ambiguously (a 1 with a flag that reads as 7's cousin, a 4 open at the top) is scored as the reader honestly read it, and a transcription slip convicts a correct poster or acquits a broken one; the two blind reads are independent, which is the only guard. The editor's repair faces the known edit-model limit: asked to change one cell, Muse Edit repaints the whole poster, and a digit that was right can drift while the broken one is fixed - the re-audit exists to catch exactly that trade, and it is reported as read. Uniqueness is load-bearing and is a property of the menu, not of the flow: the four puzzles were verified exhaustively (every completion enumerated by a brute-force solver) before being carded, so a director who copied a grid wrong would break the proof, not just the puzzle - which is why the auditor also scores the givens against the card, and a corrupted card shows up as givens the poster cannot keep. And the flow audits a 4x4 Sudoku, deliberately: sixteen cells are what a painter can draw legibly and a reader can transcribe cell by cell; the 9x9 game is left to humans and their pencils.
