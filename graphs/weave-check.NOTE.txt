# recipe-note
Plain weave is the oldest pattern law there is: at every crossing exactly one strand family lies on top, and the top family flips at every step - right, down, right, down - across the whole cloth. It is a parity law, and it is merciless: one crossing woven the wrong way breaks the alternation with up to four neighbours at once. Image models paint beautiful "woven" textures that obey it nowhere - the same strand rides on top for three crossings in a row, families swap colours mid-frame, a sixth warp strand wanders in at the edge. This flow makes the law a planned, measurable quantity - and then it does something about a violation. Give it a swatch setting and a GLM director picks one swatch from a short menu printed in its own prompt (RUST AND CREAM, INDIGO AND OCHRE, FOREST AND SKY, CHARCOAL AND ROSE) - a warp family (vertical strands, one colour and material, exactly 5), a weft family (horizontal, a contrasting colour and material, exactly 4), and a START state, which family lies on top at the top-left crossing - and copies it onto a WEAVE CARD with the full 4-row by 5-column over/under grid the parity law forces. A second GLM turns the card into one macro-photograph prompt, spelling the grid out crossing by crossing: the over strand at each crossing visibly continuous, the under strand diving beneath it. Muse paints it. A blind Gemini reader, who never saw the card, reports each family's colour and strand count and which family lies on top at every one of the 20 crossings, row by row. A GLM auditor recomputes the parity grid from the card's start state and scores PARITY cell by cell, START, WARP and WEFT, and EXTRA. A Decide judge headlines round 1: weaves true, parity slips, families swapped, strands miscounted, or not a weave.

That verdict is not where this flow stops - the loop closes, per the standing rule that a created asset ends in the corrected asset, not in text. A GLM editor reads the card, the read and the audit and writes ONE concrete edit instruction: the full target end state (the card's two families at their carded counts, plain weave alternating from the carded start corner) plus the audit's specific failures as crossing-level demands - at row 2 column 3, bring the vertical warp strand up over the horizontal weft strand. The editor is gated in content, a rule this catalog learned the hard way from Molecule Check, whose flawless round-1 model was recoloured by a repair it never needed: on a perfect audit the editor must demand a verbatim unchanged copy and forbid anything being moved, added, recoloured or rewoven. Muse Edit applies the notes to the round-1 picture. The corrected swatch is the flow's deliverable. A second blind reader re-inspects it, and the same auditor scores round 2 against the same card, so the fix is itself audited and the reader can see exactly what the edit bought - or cost. The Decide verdict stays, demoted to a side output on round 1.

Why this axis is new: the catalog's nearest neighbours are knot-check and braille-check, and the differences are mechanical. Knot-check scores over/under at crossings along ONE closed strand, traced in order through irregular zones - a flipped crossing there is a local event on one path. Braille-check reads a fixed grid of isolated dots, present or absent - a wrong dot is local to its cell. Weave check's crossings form a regular two-dimensional lattice of two orthogonal strand families, and the correct grid is not twenty independent facts but one parity rule applied in two axes: flip at every step right, flip at every step down. A single wrong crossing therefore breaks alternation with its right, left, upper and lower neighbours simultaneously, a failure shape neither a traced knot nor a dot grid can even state. And the reader's job is different in kind: not presence (braille) and not one strand's continuity (knot), but depth order at every lattice point - which of two crossing strands lies on top.

Honest limits, in the open: the reader judges depth order by eye from the photograph - at a genuinely ambiguous crossing, where the painter melted the two strands together, it must answer ?, and a ? never scores as a match, so melted crossings cost parity points even when a human might guess them right. The grid is deliberately small (5 by 4) because strand counts and crossing reads degrade fast as the lattice grows; this flow tests the law, not the reader's stamina. Only plain weave is tested - twill's diagonal step pattern and satin's long floats are other laws, deliberately out of scope. One repair round is unrolled, not a loop - if round 2 still slips parity, the flow shows the slip rather than spending again.
