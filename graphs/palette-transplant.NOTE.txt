# recipe-note
Every colour-transfer tool in the ComfyUI world — Extract Color Palette, ColorMatch, the PaletteOptimalTransport family — moves colour pixel-to-pixel onto the same image: same scene, new grade. This flow does the stranger thing the multimodel chain makes possible: it moves colour, and only colour, into a different scene that does not exist yet. Muse paints a source scene from your brief. A Gemini palette clerk measures the finished picture into a PALETTE CARD — exactly six swatches, most dominant first, each a plain colour name, an approximate hex and the share of the picture it covers, plus a LIGHT line (warm/cool, bright/muted/deep). That card is the only thing that crosses. A GLM palette director takes the card and your second, completely unrelated scene brief, and writes one painting prompt that assigns every swatch to elements of the new scene roughly in proportion to its share, repeats the hex and name of the two largest swatches, and explicitly bans the colours the new scene would naturally have when they are not on the card — for a harbour, the blue sky and grey-blue water an image model will reach for by default. Muse then paints the target scene from that prompt alone, as a fresh text-to-image: it never sees the source picture, its description, or an edit canvas, so nothing but colour can leak through. Afterwards the same clerk measures the new picture blind — it never sees the source card — into a second card in the same format. A GLM palette auditor matches the two cards swatch by swatch: CROSSED (same family, share within about fifteen points), SHIFTED (family present, share or shade clearly moved) or MISSING, then lists any INTRUDER colours in the target card whose family appears nowhere in the source, and ends in "X of 6 swatches crossed". A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the new scene and both cards and gives the headline verdict: **palette transplanted**, **palette partly crossed**, **colours of its own**, or **source scene in disguise**.

Why the loop matters: "paint this harbour in the colours of that forest" is normally graded by squinting. Here both ends of the transplant are measurements taken from finished pixels by the same clerk in the same format, so the score is a comparison of two measurements, not an opinion about one picture — and a miss is attributable: the clerk mismeasured the source, the director misassigned the swatches, the painter reached for its default colours anyway (the intruder line names them), or the re-measurement drifted. The nearest catalog neighbour, words-only repaint, runs the opposite bottleneck: a vision model describes a photo exhaustively and a painter recreates the same scene from words alone, scored on what survived. Here the description is refused — subject, composition and setting are deliberately severed, a different scene is specified independently, and the one channel allowed through is colour, carried as numbers. Decide's fourth option exists because the leak is real: a director prompt that smuggles source content ("maple-red boats under maple trees") would defeat the point, and the judge is asked to catch it.

Honest limits, in the open: a vision model is not a colorimeter. Its hex values are educated guesses and its shares are eyeball estimates, so the audit compares colour families and rough proportions, not exact values — two adjacent browns or a gold read as amber are judgement calls the SHIFTED grade exists to absorb. The painter is a text-to-image model following a prompt, not a colour-transfer node: it cannot be forced to spend exactly 30 percent of the canvas on one swatch, and its own taste in skies and water is the constant headwind the ban line pushes against; expect shares to land approximately, and read a high CROSSED count with the intruder line, not instead of it. Six swatches is a deliberate simplification — a source scene with a subtle gradient sky will be flattened into one or two names, and the transplant inherits that flattening. The audit certifies that the measured palette crossed, not that the pairing is beautiful; an autumn forest's colours on a harbour can be faithful and still be an acquired taste.

Live proof, 2026-11-14 — SUCCESS end to end on the first attempt, all nodes done, no errors, $0.0293 balance-verified. Muse's source was a peak-colour maple forest (crimson and burnt-orange canopy, golden leaf carpet, the moss-green stone lantern on the path). The clerk carded it burnt orange 25%, crimson 22%, gold 20%, bark brown 15%, peach haze 12% (the brief's cream mist, read warm in the amber light), moss green 6%, LIGHT warm/bright. The director assigned the swatches to the harbour — burnt-orange sky with crimson clouds, gold water, bark-brown boats/pier/lighthouse/crates, peach-haze gulls, moss-green nets — and banned blue sky, grey-blue water and white gulls outright. Muse's harbour honours all of it: there is no blue anywhere in the picture, and the two scenes share a palette while sharing no subject, object or setting. The blind re-measurement came back amber gold 30%, burnt orange 24%, crimson 19%, bark brown 16%, warm apricot 7%, moss green 4%. The judges split honestly: Decide returned 'palette transplanted' while the GLM audit scored 3 of 6 CROSSED and 3 SHIFTED (gold renamed amber gold and grown 20→30, peach haze renamed warm apricot at 12→7, moss green's shade deepened) with 0 MISSING and INTRUDERS: none — the families all crossed; the argument is over shares and shade names, exactly the colorimeter limit disclosed above. Both pictures are visually verified.
