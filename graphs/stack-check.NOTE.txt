# recipe-note
A tower of stacked objects can look perfect and stand for nothing: every object present, every colour right — and the third one floating a centimetre above the second, so the top three are held up by nobody. Object-by-object audits cannot see that failure, because it does not live in any object; it lives in the contacts between them. This flow builds a tower and audits its contact graph. Give it a setting (the default is a pale-grey studio wall behind a light-wood tabletop, seen straight from the side) and a GLM director writes a STACK CARD: exactly 6 different stackable objects, numbered bottom to top, each with a distinct colour, a WIDTH category (wide, medium or narrow) that never increases going up — so the planned tower is stable by construction — and a RESTS ON line naming the object directly below it, with level 1 resting on TABLE. A second GLM turns the card into a painter prompt for a flat side elevation — one ground line, every object resting directly on the one below with broad visible contact — and Muse paints the tower. A blind Gemini surveyor, who never saw the card, inventories the rendered tower bottom to top: for each object, its colour, what it RESTS ON SEEN (the object it actually touches beneath it, TABLE, or NOTHING if it floats) and its CONTACT grade (full, partial or none). A GLM auditor then runs three scores: IDENTITY, X of 6 levels matching the card; SUPPORT, X of 6 whose seen supporter matches the card and whose contact is not none; and GROUNDED, a transitive closure computed over the survey alone — level 1 is grounded only if it rests on the table, and a higher level is grounded only if its seen supporter is the level directly below it AND that level is itself grounded, so one break ungrounds every level above it — plus the FIRST BREAK level and a FLOATING list. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **stack stands**, **support drifted**, **pieces floating**, or **different stack**.

Why the loop matters: the catalog's other physical audit, shadow check, measures each object independently — a shadow's direction and length ratio per object, checked against an expectation derived from the sun; no object's score depends on any other object, so a scene can pass object by object while being globally impossible. Maze solve is the catalog's other transitive audit, but its simulation walks a path through a text grid the architect itself authored; here the graph being closed over is extracted from pixels by a blind reader, so the closure tests the render, not the plan. Support is also the third leg of the classic generative-physics trio: the physical-consistency literature lists shadow, reflection and support violations side by side as the tell-tale implausibilities of generated images — this catalog already audits shadows (shadow check) and reflections (mirror check); stack check is the missing support leg. And the cascade is the point: a level can pass its own local support check — it rests squarely on something — and still be ungrounded, because what it rests on rests on nothing. No per-object score can express that; the GROUNDED line can, and it names the exact level where the chain broke.

Honest limits, in the open: contact is judged from one flat side view, so an object hidden behind the tower or touching only at the back edge is invisible to the surveyor — the flow says the tower is a single file of six large objects precisely so every contact is on the silhouette. CONTACT is categorical (full / partial / none), not a measurement; a hairline gap and a clear float both land on 'none' only if the surveyor can see daylight, and a gap too small to see will be read as contact — the audit catches visible physics failures, not sub-pixel ones. The width rule makes the PLAN stable by construction, but the audit does not score the render's widths: a painter that narrows the base and widens the top while keeping every contact would pass support and grounded while looking precarious, and that centre-of-mass question is a different audit (shadow check's measured ratios are the nearer tool). Finally, the surveyor's supporter naming is the chain's weak link — if it misnames one object, the closure breaks at that level even on a sound tower; the IDENTITY and SUPPORT scores sit next to GROUNDED precisely so a naming break can be told apart from a physics break.

Live proof (2026-11-15, flow-hunt 18:30 session): ONE live run, SUCCESS end to end, all nodes done, no errors, no staging spend — $0.0149 balance-verified ($45.9395 → $45.9246), exactly the metered sum (Muse tower $0.011, Gemini surveyor $0.00391, GLM calls $0.00; Decide unpriced in the report, cost_exact=false). The director's card: oak-brown wooden crate (wide, on TABLE), steel-blue metal lunchbox (wide), deep-red hardcover book (medium), forest-green storage tin (medium), cream-white ceramic platter (narrow), terracotta-orange small plant pot (narrow) — widths never increasing upward, as required. Muse's render is visually verified: exactly those six objects in that order on the light-wood table against the pale-grey wall, every object resting flat on the one below, no gaps, no extras, no people. The blind surveyor read the chain back object for object — crate on TABLE, lunchbox on crate, book on lunchbox, tin on book, platter on tin, pot on platter, CONTACT full at all six levels, EXTRA OBJECTS none, TOWER COUNT 6 — and the auditor scored IDENTITY 6 of 6, SUPPORT 6 of 6, GROUNDED 6 of 6, FIRST BREAK none, FLOATING none; Decide v1.2 returned 'stack stands', a clean sweep the pixels agree with. Two honest catches, both naming looseness the auditor's near-name rule absorbed: the surveyor called the level-2 lunchbox a 'blue metal box' and the level-5 ceramic platter a 'saucer' — on the pixels the platter really is a shallow saucer-like plate, so the render, not the reader, chose the flatter form; a stricter auditor could have docked that level's identity point. And the widths are unscored by design (see the limits above): the rendered lunchbox is in fact slightly narrower than the crate beneath it, which the card allowed (wide on wide, rendered narrower) but no line in the audit checks.
