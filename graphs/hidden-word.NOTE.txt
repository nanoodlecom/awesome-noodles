# recipe-note
A hidden word is the oldest parlour code there is — a rebus with the pictures left in and the letters taken out. Give this flow a word (the default is DREAM) and a GLM coder hides it in plain sight: exactly one object per letter, in order, each chosen so its most common everyday name starts with that letter — a drum for D, a rabbit for R — plus a SETTING line for the shelf they will live on. The coder's hardest rule is about names, not things: never pick an object people commonly call by a different name starting with another letter (no sofa smuggled in for C, no cup for M), because the code lives in what the objects are *called*. A second GLM compresses the plan into one still-life prompt — the coded objects alone in a single row on a wooden shelf, in exact left-to-right order, evenly spaced, front-on, no text, no letters, no labels anywhere — and the prompt never mentions the word, the letters, or that anything is a code. Muse paints the shelf. Then a Gemini reader who has never seen the word or the plan names the shelf objects strictly left to right, by their most common names, and a GLM decoder takes the first letters of the reader's names in order, spells the DECODED word out loud, and scores the plan letter by letter: KEPT, RENAMED (the same object, but the reader's name for it starts with a different letter), WRONG OBJECT, or MISSING, plus any EXTRA objects, ending in "X of N letters survived". A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all three texts and gives the headline verdict: **spells it exactly**, **one letter lost**, **word scrambled**, or **no word there**.

Why the loop matters: every other flow in this catalog moves information as a depiction, a description or a transcription — words-only-repaint pushes a whole scene through a vision description and repaints it, bookshelf-next-read audits lettering an image model physically rendered. This one moves a message through a purely symbolic channel: object *choice*. The finished picture contains no text and nothing to OCR; the word is not drawn anywhere in it, it is encoded in the sequence of initials, and it only exists when a reader names the objects in order. That makes a failure class scoreable here that no other catalog audit can even express — synonym drift. The painter can render the planned mug perfectly and the letter still dies, because the blind reader calls it a cup: the render was right, the *vocabulary* failed. The encoder's real job is therefore picking objects whose common name is stable across models and people, and the decode audit is what checks that stability, position by position, with the blame correctly split between the painter (WRONG OBJECT, MISSING) and the shared language (RENAMED).

Honest limits, in the open: the code is only as stable as everyday naming, and naming is genuinely contestable — a reader who says "bunny" has killed an R as surely as one who says "cup", and reasonable people will disagree about which name is "most common" for borderline objects; the audit records the reader's actual name so you can judge the call yourself. Order is the second fragility: the decode reads position from left-to-right placement on one shelf row, which is why the prompt constrains the scene to a single front-on row — a painter who stacks, overlaps or wraps the objects scrambles the word for geometric reasons that have nothing to do with naming. Extra decorative objects are a third: anything else the painter sets on the shelf inserts a letter, so the prompt bans shelf clutter and the audit's EXTRA line catches what slipped through. And short words are easy mode by design — five letters is enough that a perfect score means the whole chain (stable code, faithful render, honest naming) held, few enough that you can decode the shelf yourself and check the judges' work.

Live proof, 2026-11-14 — SUCCESS end to end on the first attempt, all nodes done, no errors, $0.0225 balance-verified. The coder hid DREAM as Drum (small red toy drum), Radio (retro silver radio), Egg timer (white, hourglass-shaped), Anchor (miniature brass) and Mortar and pestle (grey stone), on a warm oak bookshelf beside a reading nook. Muse's shelf is visually verified: exactly those five objects in a single front-on row, in plan order, nothing else on the shelf, no text anywhere. The blind reader named them Drum, Radio, Egg timer, Anchor, Mortar and pestle — the decoder spelled DREAM, 5 of 5 letters KEPT, EXTRA none, and Decide returned 'spells it exactly'. One honest naming call sits inside that perfect score, and it is the flow's own failure class arriving in its mildest form: the E object on the pixels is, plainly, an hourglass — the plan itself described the egg timer as "hourglass-shaped", the painter rendered the shape faithfully, and the reader chose the plan-side name "egg timer" over "hourglass". Had it said hourglass, the word would have decoded DRAAM-adjacent nonsense and E would have scored RENAMED-by-shape; the texts cannot tell you which name is "right", which is exactly the contestability the limits above disclose — here it broke in the code's favour.
