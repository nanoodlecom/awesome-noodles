# recipe-note
Some pictures only work from one distance. A mosaic flat lay is a carpet of coffee beans up close and a heart from across the room — and a generated version can fail at either scale independently: the beans can be wrong while the heart is perfect, or the close-up can be flawless while the shape dissolves into noise the moment you squint. No single reading can catch both failures, because the two readings need different pixels. This flow reads one picture twice, at two resolutions, and audits the separation between the scales. Give it a surface (the default is a pale linen tablecloth seen from above) and a GLM director writes a SCALE CARD: one FAR SHAPE from a short list (heart, five-pointed star, crescent moon, diamond, circle, triangle), one MATERIAL of small uniform objects, a dark SHAPE COLOUR those objects take where they form the shape, and a pale FILL COLOUR of the same material everywhere else. A second GLM turns the card into a painter prompt — a dense, even, top-down carpet of the objects, the dark ones forming one large centred shape about two thirds of the frame wide — and Muse paints it. Then the picture forks. The close reader, a blind Gemini, receives the full-resolution image and inventories the objects themselves: material, both colours, coverage, and what the dark objects seem to be doing. The far reader, a second blind Gemini, receives the same picture after a free local Resize node shrinks it to 64 pixels wide — the squint view, where individual objects should be impossible to make out and only the large colour mass survives — and names the one big shape it sees, plus any individual objects it can still make out. A GLM auditor scores both readings against the card: FAR match, wrong shape or no shape; CLOSE material and colours, X of 3; and SEPARATION — clean if the far reader saw no individual objects, leaked if it could still name them. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the full picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **both distances read**, **far shape lost**, **close detail lost**, or **neither distance reads**.

Why the loop matters: the catalog already reads one picture twice (mirror check), but its two readers split one frame by region — shore versus reflection — on identical pixels, with the instruction doing the separating. Here a local transform does the separating: the far reader's pixels are physically different, low-passed by the resize, so the audit measures a property of the image itself — whether its information is genuinely organised by scale — and not whether two prompts can partition a scene. Hidden word is the other near relative: there, objects encode a message semantically, through their initial letters, and the code is read at the same resolution it was written. A squint shape is encoded geometrically, in the arrangement's large-scale luminance, and is in principle invisible at the resolution where the objects are legible — that mutual exclusivity is exactly what the SEPARATION line scores, a failure class (the scale leak) that neither a list audit nor a same-resolution code audit can express.

Honest limits, in the open: the far view is a 64-pixel fit-resize, a fair model of squinting but not of walking across a room — it removes fine detail without the contrast and glare changes of real distance, and the flow says so rather than pretending otherwise. The shape list is deliberately short and bold, because a shape that needs a paragraph to describe cannot survive 64 pixels; subtlety is traded for a reading that can actually be scored. The close reader is asked what the dark objects seem to be forming as a courtesy line, but the audit never scores it — the close reader is allowed to notice the shape; what is scored is the material and colours it was actually sent to check, and the far reader's independent naming. And a painter can cheat the scale separation by drawing the shape's outline as a continuous dark line instead of building it from objects; the close reader's coverage and material lines are the tell (a drawn outline is not a carpet of objects), and the auditor's CLOSE score is where that cheat lands.

Live proof (2026-11-15, flow-hunt 14:30 session): ONE live run, SUCCESS end to end, all nodes done, no errors, no staging spend — $0.0145 balance-verified, exactly the metered sum (Muse flat lay $0.011, Gemini close read $0.00212, Gemini far read $0.00141, GLM calls $0.00; Decide unpriced in the report, cost_exact=false). The director's card: FAR SHAPE heart, SHAPE COLOUR deep burgundy, MATERIAL dried red kidney beans, FILL COLOUR pale cream, on the default pale linen. Muse's render is visually verified: a dense, even carpet of beans wall to wall, the burgundy kidney beans forming one large clean heart about two thirds of the frame wide, cream beans everywhere else, no bare patches, no other objects. The close reader inventoried beans, dark red, fill 'white', coverage dense and even, and its courtesy arrangement line noted the heart (allowed and unscored, as designed). The far reader, on the actual 64x64-pixel derivative the Resize node produced (saved with the run), named SHAPE SEEN: heart, dark red on beige, OBJECTS SEEN: none — at 64 pixels the beans really are gone and only the heart survives, which a human look at the tiny file confirms. The auditor scored FAR: MATCH, CLOSE: 3 of 3, DRIFTED: none, SEPARATION: CLEAN, and Decide v1.2 returned 'both distances read' — a clean sweep the pixels agree with. Two honest catches, both colour-naming looseness the auditor's near-name rule absorbed: the close reader called the pale-cream fill 'white' and the far reader called the background 'beige'; on the pixels both are the same cream beans, but a stricter auditor could have docked the fill point, and a future version may want the card's colours pinned to a fixed palette of names so the near-name rule has less to forgive.
