# recipe-note
A line-up of familiar objects can look perfect and be quietly impossible: every object present, every colour right — and the cat drawn the size of the suitcase, the bicycle shrunk toward the armchair, everything regressed toward one typical size. Presence audits cannot see that failure, because it does not live in whether an object is there; it lives in how tall it stands next to the others. This flow builds a line-up and audits its proportions against the real world. Give it a setting (the default is a pale-grey studio wall behind a flat pale floor, seen straight from the side — a height-chart backdrop) and a GLM director writes a SCALE CARD: exactly 6 objects chosen from a menu of 12 familiar rigid objects with canonical real-world heights in centimetres (bookshelf 200, refrigerator 185, floor lamp 160, potted olive tree 140, bicycle 105, guitar 100, armchair 90, suitcase 70, medium dog 55, stool 45, sitting cat 40, watering can 30), the chosen heights pairwise at least 20 cm apart, a distinct colour each, ordered tallest to shortest. A second GLM turns the card into a painter prompt for a flat side elevation — all six in one row on one ground line, side by side, no overlap and no depth staggering — stating each object's height as a plain comparison with the tallest, and Muse paints the line-up. A blind Gemini surveyor, who never saw the card, measures each rendered object's height in the picture as a percentage of the tallest object's picture height (tallest = 100). A GLM auditor computes each card object's EXPECTED SHARE from the centimetres (its height ÷ the tallest height × 100) and runs four scores: IDENTITY, X of 6 objects found with matching colours; SCALE, X of 6 whose seen share lands within 15 points of expected; ORDER, X of 5 adjacent tallest-to-shortest pairs that keep their order in the seen shares; and REGRESSION — present when at least one object from the shorter half is oversized beyond the band while at least one from the upper half is undersized beyond it, the small swelling and the large shrinking in the same picture. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **true to scale**, **sizes drifted**, **mean regression**, or **wrong line-up**.

Why the loop matters: relative object scale is a distinct, measurable capability that most evaluations never isolate — the GenScale benchmark (NeurIPS 2026) was built precisely because generators render familiar objects at mutually implausible proportions (its stock example is a laptop larger than a refrigerator) while passing presence and attribute checks, and it documents the directional signature behind the failure, mean regression: intrinsically small objects are inflated and large ones shrunk toward a typical visual size. No audit in this catalog holds a render against real-world dimensions: shadow check measures each object against its own shadow under one global sun parameter, stack check extracts a contact graph, bar chart reads back authored numbers. Here the expectations are authored as physics — centimetres from the real world — and the headline diagnostic is not a pass count but the direction pattern of the signed errors, so a picture can fail informatively (mean regression) instead of just failing. The auditor also keeps an ordinal score, ORDER, alongside the banded one, following GenScale's own protocol choice: when absolute estimates are mushy, whether the ranking survived is the robust signal.

Honest limits, in the open: the surveyor's percentages are visual estimates, not pixel measurements — expect ±10 points of reader noise on a good day, which is why the band is a wide ±15 and why ORDER sits next to SCALE; a stricter band would mostly measure the reader. The flat one-ground-line staging is a controlled design, not a look: under perspective or depth staggering, picture height stops tracking real height (GenScale notes generators even exploit staggering to conceal scale errors), so the flow removes both confounds by construction and says nothing about scale in natural scenes. The tallest object is the anchor — the surveyor defines it as 100 — so the anchor's own absolute error is invisible by construction; every score is relative to it, and if the painter shrinks the whole line-up uniformly the audit cannot tell. Canonical heights are typical values, not laws: real bicycles, dogs and bookshelves vary, and the 20 cm separation rule keeps the menu's typical values from colliding, but a breed-true dog at the edge of its range could be scored drifted for the renderer's honesty. Finally, the auditor's arithmetic is an LLM's: it divides six heights by the tallest and rounds — the divisions are simple and the card carries the raw centimetres for anyone to recheck, but a slipped division would move an expected share, which is why the OFF BY line exposes every signed difference instead of hiding behind the totals.

Live proof (2026-11-15, flow-hunt 22:30 session): ONE live run, SUCCESS end to end, all nodes done, no errors, no staging spend — $0.0161 balance-verified ($45.9246 → $45.9085), exactly the metered sum (Muse line-up $0.011, Gemini surveyor $0.00506, GLM calls $0.00; Decide unpriced in the report, cost_exact=false). The director's card: walnut brown bookshelf 200 cm, brushed brass floor lamp 160, sage-green potted olive tree 140, cherry red bicycle 105, charcoal grey suitcase 70, mustard yellow stool 45 — pairwise gaps 40/20/35/35/25, all inside the rules. Muse's render is visually verified: exactly those six objects in card order on one ground line against the pale-grey wall, no extras, no people, no text. The blind surveyor read back bookcase 100, floor lamp 72, potted plant 62, bicycle 38, suitcase 30, stool 21 against expected shares 100/80/70/53/35/23, and the auditor scored IDENTITY 6 of 6, SCALE 6 of 6, ORDER 5 of 5, REGRESSION none; Decide v1.2 returned 'true to scale'. The honest catch is in the signed errors, and a human pixel check agrees with the surveyor's numbers: every non-anchor object measured UNDER its expected share (0, −8, −8, −15, −5, −2) — the painter shrank the whole line-up toward the floor, most on the bicycle, whose handlebars sit visibly below half the bookshelf's height and whose −15 lands exactly on the band's edge, so 'true to scale' inherits the ±15 band's generosity and a ±10 band would have failed the bicycle alone. The pattern is a uniform shrink, not mean regression — the small end (suitcase −5, stool −2) stayed honest while the middle ranks sagged — which is exactly the distinction the REGRESSION line exists to draw. One unscored flaw: the suitcase stands slightly in front of the bicycle's front wheel, a small overlap the brief forbade and no score counts.
