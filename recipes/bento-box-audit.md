## Notes

Bento inspiration stops at the photograph: a beautiful box, no plan behind it, and no way to pack the same lunch twice. Meal-prep lists go the other way — six foods written down, no picture, and no check that the box anyone actually packed matches the list. This graph does both halves and then closes a loop neither closes: a GLM packer writes a 6-line pack plan from a brief — one food per compartment in a 2-by-3 box, compartments named in reading order (top-left through bottom-right), each food with an exact count, honouring the brief's hard constraints (the default brief is vegetarian and bans nuts outright) — a second GLM turns the plan into a photo brief, and Muse renders the finished box from directly overhead. Then a Gemini vision bench checker inventories only what is actually visible, compartment by compartment with counts, a GLM auditor scores the original plan against that inventory line by line (PLACED / WRONG COMPARTMENT / WRONG COUNT / MISSING, EXTRA lines for anything unplanned, ending in an "X of 6 placed" summary), and a ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) gives the headline verdict: **built to plan**, **counts drifted**, **pieces missing**, or **extras snuck in**. The render is not decoration here; it is the claim, and two independent reads hold it to the plan.

Why the loop matters: image models are enthusiastic packers. They fill every compartment generously, swap two neighbours because the colours sit prettier that way, drop the fiddly counted item (the four maki become a vague cluster), and slip a photogenic garnish into a corner nobody planned. A plan alone cannot catch that, and a render alone looks convincing precisely when it is wrong. Splitting the jobs across four model families is what makes the check honest: the model that wrote the plan is not the model that rendered it, and neither of them grades it. The compartment rule is the bento-specific twist: a food can be present at exactly the right count and the box is still packed wrong, because lunch is eaten compartment by compartment — the sweet thing swapped into the savoury corner is a different lunch.

Honest limits, in the open: the bench check reads a 512 px shrink of the render, so pieces half-hidden under a neighbour are judgement calls, and the checker's "(?)" guesses flow straight into the audit — read the WRONG COUNT and WRONG COMPARTMENT lines against the render yourself before you trust them. Food identification from a render is also fallible: two pale rolled items in near colours can swap names between the render and the read-back, which becomes a false MISSING plus a false EXTRA. The auditor is a text comparison of two lists, so a colour the vision checker misreads becomes a false WRONG COUNT. And the render is a goal picture, not a photograph of a lunch anyone packed: it proves the plan fits one box and one palette, not that your rice will hold its triangles. And 6 lines is a design choice, not a law — edit the packer's system prompt and the auditor's summary line together if you build bigger boxes.

Expected cost per run: Muse text-to-image $0.011, the Gemini bench check roughly $0.001–0.008, the three GLM calls effectively $0.00, and the Decide audit is not priced in the 2026-09-12 catalog snapshot — expect roughly $0.02–0.04 all in. Prices are observations, not caps.

Live proof, 2026-11-10: the full chain ran end to end for $0.0141 balance-verified ($28.8883 → $28.8743, exactly the metered sum once the bench check's delayed billing settled; Muse render $0.011, Gemini bench check $0.00307, GLM calls $0.00, Decide unpriced in the report), no staging input needed — the bento brief is text. The packer honoured every hard constraint (vegetarian, no nuts, no liquids, six different foods) and planned 4 herb-flecked vegetable onigiri top-left, 5 cherry tomatoes top-middle, 3 broccoli florets top-right, 4 roasted beet wedges bottom-left, 3 tamagoyaki slices bottom-middle and 5 roasted sweet potato cubes bottom-right. Muse's render is visually verified: exactly that box, matte black lacquer on pale linen, every food in its planned compartment, nothing outside the compartments, no utensils or text. Both judges agree it is close but not exact: Decide returned **counts drifted** and the audit scored **4 of 6 placed** — the honest flaws are two single-piece count drifts, both real on the pixels: the renderer packed 6 cherry tomatoes against 5 planned, and 4 sweet potato pieces against 5. No compartment was wrong and nothing extra appeared, which is the compartment system's best result so far in this catalog family: placement held perfectly, only generosity drifted. Keep the auditor wired to both the packer's plan and the bench check, or the score stops meaning anything; swap the render model in the Bento node's ⚙️ settings if you prefer a different photographic look.
