## Notes

Pet-portrait apps do one trick: upload a photo, pick a style filter, get a painting of *a* cat or *a* dog — and the classic failure is that the painting is lovely and the pet is gone: the patch over the left eye migrates, the coat pattern smooths into generic tabby, the green eyes go amber. Nothing in those apps ever checks the portrait against the animal. This graph splits the jobs across four model families and closes that loop: a Gemini vision node is the identity clerk (it writes an identity card — species guess, then a numbered list of 5 to 8 identity marks with side and position, then a temperament read from the pose), a GLM node changes hats into an art historian (it picks ONE historical painting style because it suits that temperament, and its plan must repeat every numbered mark on a "Keep exactly:" line), a second GLM compresses the plan into a single surgical edit instruction that starts with the keep-list, and Muse Edit paints the portrait from the original photo itself, not from a text description of it.

Then the portrait has to answer for itself, twice. A second Gemini vision node reads the painting back and inventories only the marks actually visible in it — it never sees the card, so it cannot grade generously — a GLM likeness auditor scores the card against that read-back mark by mark (KEPT / CHANGED / LOST, ending in an "X of Y identity marks kept" summary), and a ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) gives the headline verdict from the card plus the portrait image: **same pet, painted**, **markings drifted**, **different animal**, or **still a photo**. The graph exports the **Portrait — audited** verdict text, and the portrait itself rides through the Decide node as its image output — open the Portrait node to see the painting the verdict is about.

Why the split matters: the model that painted the portrait is not the model that wrote the identity card, and neither of them grades the likeness. The card is written *before* any style is chosen, so the style cannot quietly redefine what counts as the pet; the read-back is written *without* the card, so the auditor compares two independent reads instead of one model's opinion of its own work.

Honest limits, in the open: the identity card is a photo read, not ground truth — the clerk can misread a marking's side, and every downstream check then faithfully defends the wrong card, so read the card before you trust the audit. The read-back reads a shrunk copy of the portrait, so fine marks (a small scar, a tag's engraving) are judgement calls, and the "( ? )" guesses flow straight into the score. Muse Edit re-renders rather than paints over, so pose and framing can drift even when every mark survives. And a generated staging photo was used for the live proof below — no real pet photo was staged in this workspace — disclosed here rather than smoothed over.

Expected cost per run: Muse Edit $0.011, the two Gemini vision reads roughly $0.005–0.007 each, the three GLM calls effectively $0.00–0.011 combined, and the Decide audit is not priced in the 2026-09-12 catalog snapshot — expect roughly $0.03–0.05 all in. Prices are observations, not caps.

Live proof, 2026-11-08: the full chain ran end to end for $0.0452 balance-verified across two spends ($42.7789 → $42.7337) — a generated staging photo of a split-face calico cat ($0.0110 exact, Muse; no real pet photo was staged, as disclosed above), then ONE live run of the graph ($0.0342 metered: identity read $0.00506, historian $0.00183, edit brief $0.00490, Muse Edit $0.011, read-back $0.00601, likeness audit $0.00543; the Decide audit unpriced in the report, ~$0.00002 by balance gap). The identity clerk nailed the staging cat: calico with white bib and paws, a sharp split-face — black on the cat's right (viewer's left), ginger tabby on the left — black and ginger ears to match, pale green eyes, pink nose, banded tail wrapped over the front paws, no collar, temperament 'poised, serene, attentive'. The historian chose Dutch Golden Age portraiture (Rembrandt school) for that temperament, on a deep umber-green ground with one wine-red drapery fold and a crimson velvet cushion with gold tassels as the single prop. Muse Edit's portrait is genuinely hang-worthy — and it is visibly the same cat: split face on the correct sides, ears, eyes, nose, bib, shoulder patches and wrapped tail all in place. The two judges then split in the honest direction, and both are recorded: the Decide headline verdict was **same pet, painted**, while the mark-by-mark audit scored **5 of 7 kept** — it marked Eyes CHANGED (the card's 'vivid pale green' vs the read-back's 'pale yellowish-green', a shade call a human eye would wave through) and Body and tail markings CHANGED (the read-back described the flank patches and tail banding in different groupings than the card). That is the loop working as designed: the detailed audit is stricter than both the headline judge and the human eye, and its two CHANGED lines are wording-and-shade strictness, not a lost pet. Run report and images: flow-hunt/runs/2026-11-08-pet-portrait-studio/ in the nanoodle-dev workspace (portrait saved as calico-dutch-golden-age-portrait.png).

Change the **Portrait brief** input for mood, era and where the portrait will hang ("playful backyard portrait, bright modern colour" sends the historian somewhere very different), and swap the pet photo for any animal — the identity card adapts, because the marks are read, not assumed. Keep the auditor wired to both the identity card and the read-back, or the score stops meaning anything; swap the render model in the Portrait node's ⚙️ settings if you prefer a different painterly hand.
