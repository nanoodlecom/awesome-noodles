## Notes

Every other picture in this catalog is about what is in it. This one is about what is not: a shape that exists only as empty sky, carved by the silhouettes around it — the bright gap between two rooftops that happens to be a heart, the sky between two cliffs that happens to be a key. Give it a setting (the default is a flat pale-cream dawn sky behind dark silhouettes on one flat horizon) and a GLM director writes a NEGATIVE CARD: one SHAPE chosen from a menu (five-pointed star, heart, crescent moon, key, diamond, bell, arrow pointing up, bird in flight), a LEFT FORM and a RIGHT FORM whose facing edges bound the shape's two sides, a FLOOR it rests above, and exactly 5 dark silhouette objects left to right, the two forms among them. The shape is specified negatively — the painter is never asked to draw it, only to place the dark forms whose edges carve it; a star drawn in the sky is a failure, not a shortcut. A second GLM turns the card into a painter prompt for a flat high-contrast silhouette scene, and Muse paints it. A blind Gemini surveyor, who never saw the card, first lists the silhouette objects left to right, then performs the figure-ground inversion: ignoring the dark objects completely, it names the one familiar shape the bright EMPTY central region itself forms — or NONE if the gap is just irregular sky — and lists anything that intrudes into that empty region. A GLM auditor scores OBJECTS, X of 5 in position; SHAPE, MATCH / NEAR / MISS against the card; and GAP, CLEAN or INTRUDED. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **shape in the sky**, **shape almost there**, **just sky**, or **wrong scene**.

Why the loop matters: the catalog's two nearest neighbours both encode patterns in what is present. Hidden word spells its word from the initials of objects that are there; squint test's picture is made of materials that are there, read at two distances. Neither audit can even state this flow's question, because an inventory of everything present leaves the centrepiece out — the shape lives in the region every object list skips. That is also why the surveyor's two passes matter: its object list proves the scene is the planned one, and only the second, inverted read can say whether the emptiness between the objects resolved into the planned shape or stayed a shapeless gap. Negative space is a designed quantity in poster and logo work precisely because viewers read it whether the designer shaped it or not; a generator that places beautiful silhouettes can leave accidental shapes — or fail to leave intended ones — in the sky between them, and no foreground audit will ever report it.

Honest limits, in the open: the figure-ground read is a single blind reader naming a shape, and shape-naming is judgement, not measurement — a ragged star-shaped gap can be read as 'star' by a generous reader or 'NONE' by a strict one, and the MATCH / NEAR / MISS band absorbs only part of that looseness. The plan constrains the shape with only three boundaries (left edge, right edge, floor), so its top is whatever the two forms' heights make of it; tall forms of uneven height will tilt or truncate shapes whose tops matter, like the star and the heart. And the audit scores the central gap only: accidental negative shapes elsewhere in the sky go unreported, as do shapes formed by the dark silhouettes' own outlines — a lighthouse that itself looks like a key is the foreground's business, not this audit's, and the surveyor is told not to name a shape just because an object looks like one.

Live proof (2026-11-16, flow-hunt 10:30 session): ONE live run, SUCCESS end to end, all nodes done, no errors, no staging spend — $0.0139 balance-verified ($45.0245 → $45.0106), exactly the metered sum (Muse scene $0.011, Gemini surveyor $0.00293, GLM calls $0.00; Decide unpriced in the report, cost_exact=false). The director carded SHAPE: heart, LEFT FORM church spire, RIGHT FORM lighthouse, FLOOR long harbour wall, objects pine tree / church spire / water tower / lighthouse / windmill. The render is visually verified: exactly those five silhouettes in order on a dark ground band against a plain pale-cream sky, no text, no extras — and a large, unmistakable heart in the sky. Two honest catches, both about HOW the heart got there. First, the painter took the forbidden shortcut this flow exists to catch: the heart is a pale tonal shape painted into the sky itself (a lighter heart visibly glowing behind the silhouettes), not a shape carved by the silhouettes' facing edges — the spire and lighthouse are straight-sided, and the heart's curved outline owes nothing to them. Second, the audit chain caught it rather than certifying it: the blind surveyor read SKY SHAPE: heart but listed INTRUSIONS as the church steeple, water tower and lighthouse — pixel-true against the painted heart, whose lower half all three silhouettes stand inside — so the auditor scored OBJECTS 5 of 5, SHAPE MATCH, GAP INTRUDED, and Decide v1.2 returned 'shape almost there', not the headline. A third, quieter flaw is the director's own: it placed the water tower (object 3) BETWEEN the two bounding forms, so even a perfectly carved gap would have had a silhouette standing in it — the card's geometry was compromised before the painter cheated. The flow's first outing thus proves the audit's teeth (a drawn shape plus intrusions is exactly the 'almost there' band) while leaving the clean sweep — a heart that is only sky, bounded by edges alone — unclaimed.
