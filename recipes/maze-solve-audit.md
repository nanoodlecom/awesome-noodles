## Notes

A maze is a global promise disguised as a local picture. Whether a corridor dead-ends in the top corner is decided by wall choices in the bottom corner, so an image model can paint a maze that looks perfect and has no way through — the Elbo Books maze project calls AI mazes "pathologically difficult" for exactly this reason, because solvability is a graph property, not a look. And the solving side is just as slippery: MazeBench (arXiv 2603.26839) found that vision models asked to solve a maze do not plan visually at all — they translate the picture into a text grid and then search in tokens, and a text-grid ablation (handing the model the correct grid) isolates weak visual extraction from downstream search. This flow builds both findings into one pipeline and audits each hop separately. Give it a theme (the default is an English garden: hedge blocks, gravel paths, a red flag at the start, a gold star at the exit) and a Gemini architect designs a formal 7×7 block maze as pure text — first a solution ROUTE written as (row, column) coordinates, then exactly 7 lines of 7 characters built from that route, `#` for a wall block, `.` for path, `S` in the top row, `E` in the bottom row, branch cells forming at least three dead ends — and converts its route into a solution as a direction sequence. A second GLM turns the grid into a single image prompt that describes the grid row by row, cell by cell, and is forbidden from mentioning the solution: the picture must show an unsolved maze. Muse paints it flat, straight down, one square. Then a Gemini solver who never saw the plan does what MazeBench says solvers do, on the record: it transcribes the rendered maze back into a 7×7 character grid, and proposes a path through its own transcription. A GLM auditor then simulates every walk step by step — the architect's solution on the plan grid (a plan whose own solution crosses a wall fails before the render is scored at all), the solver's path on the solver's grid, and the same path on the plan grid — and counts the grid match cell by cell, X of 49. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **solvable as drawn**, **maze drifted**, **solver lost**, or **no way through**.

Why the loop matters: every other audit in this catalog compares lists, counts or descriptions — things that can be checked position by position. Solvability cannot. It is a property of a whole route, and a path is only legal as a sequence: step 14 crosses a wall or not depending on where steps 1–13 left you. So this audit does not compare the solver's answer against the plan's answer (two different legal routes through the same maze would score as a mismatch); it re-walks both routes on both grids and reports where each one dies, if it dies. That dual simulation is what charges a failure to the right hop. If the solver's path is legal on its own transcription but illegal on the plan grid, the painter or the transcription drifted — the maze changed under the solver. If it is illegal on the solver's own grid, the maze was fine and the search failed. If the plan's own solution is broken, nobody downstream gets blamed. Metro-map is the nearest relative — a formal spec rendered, then read back — but its audit matches station lists along a line; nothing in the catalog simulates a walk, and nothing else gates the render's score on the plan first proving itself.

Honest limits, in the open: the maze is deliberately small and blocky — 7×7 square cells, flat top-down, no thin-wall cell maze — because that is the largest structure whose transcription stays a measurement instead of a guess; a lush illustrated maze would make the read-back mush and the audit meaningless, and the flow says so rather than pretending to score it. The auditor is itself a language model simulating walks in text: it is instructed to walk step by step and name the first illegal move, but a long path is exactly where a text simulation can slip, which is why both grids and both paths are printed in full — you can re-walk any of them yourself in a minute, and the painted maze is a playable puzzle, so your own solve is a third check no verdict here can overrule. The architect is likewise a language model: its connectivity and dead-end rules are requested and then verified only where the audit can reach — the solution walk is simulated, but nobody re-counts the dead ends, so treat that line as the architect's claim, not a scored fact. And a faithful grid with a legal solver path still leaves one thing unscored: whether the maze is *interesting*. Dead ends and route length are in the plan; elegance is yours to judge from the picture.

First live attempt, 2026-11-15 — FAILED at the audit node, and the failure is this flow's whole argument in miniature, so it is recorded in full. As first built, the architect was GLM-5.3-Flash, and it returned a grid truncated mid-row-4 with no SOLUTION line at all; isolated probes then isolated why: GLM Flash returns "no text in response" whenever it is asked to *emit* a character grid — even echoing a grid it was handed fails — while it handles grids as input perfectly well (the cartographer and the auditor both worked on grid text in the same run). The painter and the solver were blameless: Muse faithfully painted the cartographer's prompt (which, faced with half a plan, had invented the missing rows as solid hedge — the exit star sealed in its corner), and the blind solver transcribed that picture almost cell-perfectly and honestly answered NO PATH. The auditor then returned no text at all on the malformed plan. Repairs, all probe-proven the same session at a total of $0.0272: the architect is now Gemini 3.8 Flash (the model that, as solver, had just emitted a flawless grid) with a coordinates-first design — it writes the solution as a (row, column) route before drawing the grid from it, which probe F showed produces a mechanically perfect route and solution conversion — and a maxTokens of 8000, because Gemini's thinking tokens share the output cap and caps of 1200–3000 truncated its answers mid-grid in probes C–E; the auditor gained a missing-lines guard (an incomplete plan now scores as an incomplete plan, BROKEN at move 0, never silence) and a 1200 cap, proven on the failed run's exact input. One honest remainder: probe F's grid still slipped against its own perfect route in two cells (one route cell walled, the exit drawn as a plain path cell) — grid transcription is the architect's weak hop, and catching exactly that is what the plan-path gate is for. Verification run, 2026-11-15 (06:30 session) — SUCCESS end to end, all nodes done, no errors, $0.0628 balance-verified. The Gemini architect produced a complete plan (17-coordinate route, full 7×7 grid, SOLUTION of 16 moves, 4 dead ends claimed); Muse painted a clean English-garden hedge maze, red flag at the start, gold star at the exit (visually verified, and traceable by eye against the plan). The blind solver transcribed the render at 48 of 49 cells — the one drift is real on the pixels: the painter drew only a hedge fringe in row 2, column 7, where the plan walls the cell, and the solver honestly read it as path. The auditor's three simulations all landed: PLAN PATH: VALID; the solver's path — identical to the architect's own solution — REACHES EXIT in 16 moves on its own read grid and on the plan grid. Decide v1.2 headline: solvable as drawn. The one-cell drift changed nothing structural (that cell is a dead-end spur off the route, not on it), which is exactly the kind of distinction the dual simulation exists to make: a list-match audit would have scored the maze 48/49 and stopped; the walk says the promise the picture makes — a way through — survived intact.
