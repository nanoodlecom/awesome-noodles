## Notes

Packing apps split into two camps: list generators (TripPack AI, PackGoat) that stop at text, and photo scanners (PackWise, SnapPack, TripPack's luggage scanner) that read a photo of your real things and tell you what is missing. This graph does the camps in the opposite order, and closes a loop neither camp closes: a GLM packer writes a 12-item carry-on list from a trip brief — exactly 12, each item specific in colour and material, every one a physical object that can lie flat and be seen from above, no documents, nothing worn — a second GLM turns the list into a flat-lay photo brief, and Muse renders the lay-out. Then a Gemini vision checker walks the render like a floor manager and inventories only what is actually visible in it, a GLM auditor ticks the original list off against that inventory line by line (PRESENT / MISSING / EXTRA, ending in an "X of 12 present" summary), and a ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) gives the headline verdict: **packed exactly**, **one item missing**, **several missing**, or **extras snuck in**. The flat lay is not decoration here; it is the claim, and two independent reads hold it to the list.

Why the loop matters: image models are enthusiastic packers. They drop the fiddly item (the clear umbrella, the charger), merge two garments into one blob, and invent a photogenic extra nobody packed — a scarf, sunglasses, a paperback. A list alone cannot catch that, and a render alone looks convincing precisely when it is wrong. Splitting the jobs across four model families is what makes the check honest: the model that wrote the list is not the model that rendered it, and neither of them grades it.

Honest limits, in the open: the floor check reads a 512 px shrink of the flat lay, so small items (a belt buckle, a cable) are judgement calls, and the checker's "(?)" guesses flow straight into the tick-off — read the MISSING lines against the render yourself before you trust them. The auditor is a text comparison of two lists, so a colour the vision checker misreads becomes a false MISSING. The render is a plan, not a photograph of your bag: it proves the list is lay-out-able in one carry-on's worth of space, not that your real sweater is navy. And 12 items is a design choice, not a law — edit the packer's system prompt and the auditor's count line together if you pack heavier.

Expected cost per run: Muse text-to-image $0.011, the Gemini floor check roughly $0.001–0.006, the three GLM calls effectively $0.00–0.011, and the Decide audit is not priced in the 2026-09-12 catalog snapshot — expect roughly $0.02–0.04 all in. Prices are observations, not caps.

Live proof, 2026-11-08: the full chain ran end to end for $0.0245 balance-verified ($42.8087 → $42.7842; metered $0.02449, the Decide audit unpriced in the report), no staging input needed — the trip brief is text. The packer put the umbrella first and built a credible rainy-city list (yellow hooded rain shell, merino base layer and sweater, navy trousers, running tights, two pairs of shoes, laptop, charger, clear toiletry pouch). Muse's flat lay is a clean, screenshot-worthy grid with every item recognisable. The two judges then honestly disagreed, and both are recorded: the Decide headline verdict was **packed exactly**, while the tick-off scored **11 of 12** — the packer asked for 3 *pairs* of grey socks and the render laid out 3 single socks, which the floor check counted correctly and the auditor refused to round up. That is the loop working as designed: the detailed audit catches the count technicality the headline judge waves through. Run report and images: flow-hunt/runs/2026-11-08-pack-the-bag-checked/ in the nanoodle-dev workspace (flat lay saved as carry-on-flat-lay.png).

Change the **Trip brief** input for destination, weather, bag size and the things you always forget ("the charger lives by the door — list it first"), and the whole chain re-packs around it. Keep the auditor wired to both the packer's list and the floor check, or the tick-off stops meaning anything; swap the render model in the Flat lay node's ⚙️ settings if you prefer a different lay-out look.
