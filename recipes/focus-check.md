## Notes

Focus in a photograph is not a mood; it is a plane. At a wide aperture only one distance is truly sharp, and sharpness falls away on both sides of that plane - the near side softer, the far side softer still. Generators are famously deep-focus by default: they render every object in a scene with the same crisp edges, which is why AI pictures so often read as illustrations of a place rather than photographs taken in one. This flow makes the focal plane a planned, measurable quantity - and then it does something about a miss. Give it a setting (the default is any location with a long front-to-back depth row) and a GLM director writes a FOCUS CARD: one scene from a menu built for depth (garden path, harbour quay, market street, orchard row, country lane), exactly 5 objects front to back at fixed graded distances - 1, 3, 6, 10 and 15 metres - each a distinct colour, and a FOCAL object chosen from positions 2 to 4, never the nearest or farthest. A second GLM turns the card into one painter prompt that demands a shallow depth of field at f/1.8, the focal object tack sharp and every other object blurred in plain words, nearer ones softly, farther ones progressively more. Muse paints the scene. A blind Gemini surveyor, who never saw the card, grades each visible object's SHARPNESS - SHARP, SLIGHTLY SOFT, SOFT or VERY BLURRED - from edge crispness and texture, and names the single SHARPEST position (or NONE on a tie). A GLM auditor converts grades to points (3/2/1/0) and scores IDENTITY (X of 5 objects in position), FOCUS PEAK (the surveyor's sharpest position equals the card's focal number), FALL-OFF (X of 4: every non-focal object strictly softer than the focal object, with points never increasing as you move away from the focal plane on either side), and ALL SHARP (all five graded SHARP). A Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the round-1 picture and all the texts and gives a headline verdict: **focus landed**, **focus missed**, **all sharp**, or **wrong scene**.

That verdict used to be where this flow stopped. It no longer is - the loop closes. A GLM editor reads the card, the survey and the audit and writes ONE concrete edit instruction: the full target end state (focal object named and tack sharp, nearer objects softly blurred, farther objects blurred progressively more) plus the audit's specific failures as extra demands, object by object - blur the wrongly sharp one, soften the one that broke the gradient - under a hard constraint that nothing moves, is added, removed or recoloured. Muse Edit applies those notes to the original picture. The corrected picture is the flow's deliverable. A second blind Gemini surveyor then re-grades it, and the same GLM auditor scores round 2 against the same card, so the fix is itself audited: the two audits sit side by side and the reader can see exactly what the edit bought - or cost. The Decide verdict stays, but demoted to a side output on round 1; the chain ends in an asset, not in text.

Why the loop matters: the catalog's other optical audits each measure a quantity carried by position or content - scale check measures height shares, stereo check displacement between two panels, squint test whole-image legibility at a second scale. Sharpness is a quantity carried by NO object and NO position: it is a per-object texture property that only exists relative to a plane none of the objects sits visibly on. The two-sided card is the second mechanism: a simple front-to-back blur gradient is the generator's one focus trick, and it puts the peak at the front by construction - a focal object at position 3 with a blurred object NEARER than it can only appear if the painter actually placed a focal plane mid-row, which makes FOCUS PEAK a plan-fidelity measurement, not a vibes grade. And ALL SHARP names the signature failure in advance - the deep-focus default where nothing is blurred at all - a failure every beauty judge passes and no other catalog audit can even state. The fix round is the third mechanism, and it is the point of the retrofit: an audit that cannot change the picture is a report card. Here the audit's findings are phrased in the same per-object vocabulary the editor can act on (this object, this much more blur), which is what makes a one-shot repair round possible at all - the measurement and the correction share a language.

Honest limits, in the open: the surveyor's sharpness grades are a visual judgement from one reader, not a measured modulation transfer function - expect one-grade wobble on borderline objects, which is why FALL-OFF asks for a strict ordering rather than exact grades, and why a tie for sharpest is scored as a missed peak rather than rounded in the plan's favour. Distances on the card are scene directions to the painter, not surveyed metres; the audit scores position order and relative sharpness, never absolute distance. Only the five row objects are graded - background blur beyond the row is implied by the verdict but not scored separately. The fix round has its own limit, stated plainly: an edit model asked to change only sharpness can still drift the scene - an object recoloured, a texture repainted, a position nudged. That is exactly what round 2's IDENTITY line is for: if identity drops between the audits, the repair cost more than it bought, and both audits are printed so the reader can see it. One repair round is unrolled, not a loop - if round 2 still misses, the flow shows the miss rather than spending again.
