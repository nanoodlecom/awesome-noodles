## Notes

Ask an image model for a metro map and it will happily invent the network: a handsome schematic with plausible station names, a third line nobody planned, two stations swapped where nobody looks — and the map looks so authoritative that nobody rides it back to the plan. This graph makes the map answer for its network. A GLM transit planner writes a two-line network spec from a brief — an invented city title, Line A running west to east with exactly five stations, Line B running north to south with exactly five stations, and one shared interchange station that is stop 3 on both lines, named identically in both blocks — a second GLM turns the spec into a map-design brief, and Muse renders the schematic: one straight horizontal crimson line, one straight vertical teal line, crossing only at the double-ring interchange dot, every station name printed beside its dot. Then a Gemini vision map checker traces each rendered line and reads the labels back in travel order, a GLM auditor scores the spec against that read-back listing by listing (MAPPED / WRONG ORDER / WRONG LINE / MISSING across all ten listings, EXTRA lines for any station that was never in the spec, a separate INTERCHANGE - CORRECT or WRONG line, ending in an "X of 10 mapped" summary), and a ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) gives the headline verdict: **mapped to spec**, **order drifted**, **stations missing**, or **wrong network**. The map is not decoration here; it is a claim about a network — which stations, on which line, in which order, crossing where — and two independent reads hold it to that claim.

Why the loop matters: a metro map is a topology diagram wearing a poster's clothes. Its failures are not ugly pixels but wrong facts — a station on the wrong line still looks like a station, and an interchange drawn one stop early quietly rewires every journey on the network. Splitting the jobs across four model families is what makes the check honest: the model that planned the network is not the model that drew it, and neither of them traces it back. The forced crossing is the design's keystone: because the interchange must be stop 3 on both lines, the map's geometry itself encodes a checkable fact — the lines cross at the middle station or they do not — and the audit scores the topology, not the styling.

Honest limits, in the open: the map check reads a 512 px shrink of the render, so small labels crowded near the crossing are judgement calls, and the checker's "(?)" guesses flow straight into the audit — read the WRONG ORDER lines against the poster yourself before you trust them. Station names are matched loosely (a close spelling counts as the same station), so a subtly renamed label can pass. The auditor scores names, lines, order and the interchange; it does not measure geography — a station drawn at a wild spacing still counts as mapped if its label sits in the right sequence, and only the Decide judge, looking at the whole image, can catch a map that is topologically right but visually misleading. The network is invented by the planner — this proves a schematic can be made to carry a planned topology faithfully, not that any real city should build it. And two lines of five is a design choice, not a law — edit the planner's system prompt and the auditor's summary line together if your network runs longer, knowing every added label makes the render and the read-back harder. One deliberate guard from this catalog's own repair history: the map-brief node is capped (maxTokens 300, reasoningEffort low, the pairing the mural-wall repair landed on), because an uncapped brief generation on a detailed plan once ran past the HTTP timeout three runs in a row.

Expected cost per run: Muse text-to-image $0.011, the Gemini map check roughly $0.001–0.012, the three GLM calls effectively $0.00, and the Decide audit is not priced in the 2026-09-12 catalog snapshot — expect roughly $0.02–0.04 all in. Prices are observations, not caps.

Live proof, 2026-11-11: the full chain ran end to end for $0.0133 balance-verified ($23.0311 → $23.0178, exactly matching the metered $0.01334 once the map check's delayed billing settled — Muse render $0.011, Gemini map check $0.00234, GLM calls $0.00, Decide unpriced in the report), no staging input needed — the metro brief is text. The planner's network: Kestwick Metro — Line A (crimson, west to east) Wexley, Draymoor, Larkspur, Oakfield, Sunvale; Line B (teal, north to south) Tarnwick, Ashmere, Larkspur, Redbrook, Fenholm; interchange Larkspur, stop 3 on both lines. Muse's map is visually verified: exactly that cross, one horizontal crimson line and one vertical teal line meeting only at the double-ring Larkspur dot, every label in its planned order, nothing else printed but the title and the two line names. Both judges returned a clean sweep on the map's first outing: Decide **mapped to spec** and the audit scored **10 of 10 mapped** with **INTERCHANGE - CORRECT**, the read-back matching the spec name for name. One honest flaw, visible on the pixels: the Larkspur label sits directly under its oversized double-ring dot, so the crossing and the vertical line overlap the middle of the word — legible, and the checker read it, but the interchange label is the one label on the map that has to fight its own dot. Change the **Metro brief** input for city flavour and constraints ("a coastal city whose stations are named for sea things", "a university city, stations named for libraries and halls" — keep two lines, five stations each and the stop-3 interchange, or the read-back stops being a fair test), and the whole chain re-plans around it. Keep the auditor wired to both the planner's spec and the map check, or the score stops meaning anything; swap the render model in the Metro map node's ⚙️ settings if you prefer a different schematic style.
