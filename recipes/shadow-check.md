## Notes

Every object in a generated picture is lit by its own private sun. A lamp post rakes a long evening shadow to the left while the tree beside it throws a short noon shadow to the right, and the picture still looks pleasant — because nobody measures shadows, they just feel them. Shadow direction and length are not decoration, though: they are geometry. One sun at one elevation means every shadow in a flat scene falls on the same side, and each shadow's length is fixed by its caster's height — a low sun stretches shadows to about twice the caster's height, a high sun shrinks them to about half. This flow turns that rule into an audit. Give it a scene (the default is a seaside promenade in flat paper-cut style) and a GLM director writes a SUN CARD: the sun on one side, LEFT or RIGHT, at one elevation, LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH, with the shadow side and the shadow-length category the elevation dictates, plus exactly five upright objects in standing order, each with a distinctive colour and a height in whole units from 1 to 4, all on one flat ground line. A second GLM turns the card into a single painter prompt, and Muse paints the scene as a flat side elevation. A Gemini surveyor who never saw the card then measures the rendered picture — which side the sun is on, and for each object, left to right, which side its ground shadow falls on and how long that shadow is compared with the object's own height, in categories from none through about half and about the same up to about double and much longer. A GLM auditor scores the survey against the card: sun match, direction X of 5, lengths MATCH, NEAR (one category step) or OFF, and any orphan shadows that belong to no object. A ⚖️ Decide judge (PPLX Decider) sees the picture and all the texts and gives the headline verdict: **sun obeyed**, **shadows drifted**, **wrong sun**, or **no shadows**.

Why the loop matters: every other audit in this catalog compares lists, counts, descriptions or correspondences — things a picture either contains or does not. A shadow is a measurement. It can be on the right side and still be the wrong length, and two neighbouring shadows can disagree with each other even when each looks plausible alone, which is an internal inconsistency no inventory audit can express: the picture is not lying about what is in it, it is lying about the light. So this audit never asks "does the picture contain a shadow?" — it asks whether five independent measurements agree with one computed expectation, the expectation being derived from the sun's stated elevation by a fixed factor table (LOW about double, MEDIUM about the same, HIGH about half), not from anything the painter said. Mirror check is the nearest relative — one frame checked against a physical rule — but its rule is correspondence under a flip, scored as two inventories matched item by item; here there is no second inventory, only quantities, and the failure classes are graded: a shadow one category step long is NEAR, not wrong, which is why the auditor reports the band instead of a pass/fail.

Honest limits, in the open: the scene is deliberately flat — a side elevation with one horizontal ground line, five big simple silhouettes, no perspective — because in a perspective scene a ground shadow's painted length mixes the sun's geometry with the view's foreshortening and stops being a measurement; the flow says so rather than pretending to score it. The surveyor's lengths are categories, not decimals: a vision model estimating "about double" is a measurement, the same model estimating "1.83 times" would be a guess wearing a lab coat, and the categories are sized so that one step of reader wobble lands as NEAR instead of flipping a verdict. Heights are relative units within one picture, so a painter who draws the 4-unit lamp post only three times the 1-unit dog has already bent the ruler — the surveyor measures each shadow against its own caster, which keeps the ratio honest even when the absolute scale drifts. And the sun disc is part of the audit, not set dressing: if the painter puts the sun on the wrong side but aims every shadow at the card, the survey catches the disc and the verdict can name which half of the lie the picture told.

Muse's paper-cut promenade is visually verified — flat elevation, one ground line, sun a half-disc on the right horizon, all five objects in order at proportional heights — and it is a beautiful lie about the light: every shadow does fall left, but on the pixels each one measures only about half its caster's height, the HIGH-sun length, as if the painter drew the sun it felt like rather than the sun in the sky. The blind surveyor measured exactly that (sun RIGHT; five shadows all LEFT, all "about half"; no orphans), the auditor scored SUN: MATCH, DIRECTION: 5 of 5, LENGTH: 0 of 5 in band (all OFF, none NEAR — "about half" is two category steps from "about double"), and Decide v1.2 gave the honest headline: shadows drifted. One surveyor wobble, recorded for fairness: the bench's shadow is about 1.5 times the bench's height on the pixels, the top edge of "about the same", and the surveyor called it "about half" — one object read one step short, which changes nothing in the audit's outcome and is exactly the category-boundary case the NEAR band exists for when it lands nearer the plan.
