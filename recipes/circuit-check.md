## Notes

Wire four bulbs three different ways and the wiring - not the bulbs - decides how bright each one is. A bulb alone on a closed branch gets the whole battery and glows full; two bulbs in series on one branch share the power and glow dim and exactly equally; a bulb behind an open switch gets nothing and stays dark, however big and important it looks. Image models do not reason this way: left to themselves they light every bulb in a row at the same cheerful brightness, or light the biggest bulb most of all. This flow makes the brightness a derived, measurable quantity - and then it does something about a violation. Give it a bench setting (an empty circuit board) and a GLM director picks one board from a menu printed in its own prompt (WORKSHOP, GARDEN, HARBOUR, GREENHOUSE) - four bulbs in distinct coloured holders, one SOLO, one SERIES pair naming each other, one OPEN behind the board's one switch - and copies it onto a CIRCUIT CARD in left-to-right order, wiring only, with a DECOY line naming the open-switch bulb: a larger lantern globe that looks made to outshine the rest. A second GLM turns the card into a board prompt describing the battery, the holders, the wire routing and the open switch with its visible gap, plus the circuit law stated once and abstractly - solo full, series dim and equal, open dark - and is forbidden to state any individual bulb's glow state: the painter must work each state out from the wiring itself. Muse paints the board. A blind Gemini reader, who never saw the card, reports each bulb's holder colour and glow state (BRIGHT / DIM / OFF, judged by halo and light pool), the bulb count and whether the switch stands open. A GLM auditor derives every bulb's expected state from its wiring (SOLO = BRIGHT, SERIES = DIM, OPEN = OFF) and scores STATES, RANK (solo above series above open), PAIR EQUAL (the series pair must read the same state as each other - an equality constraint, not just two individual scores), SWITCH and DECOY. A Decide judge headlines round 1: wired to the rules, brightness wrong, dead bulb lit, series pair uneven, or not a circuit.

That verdict is not where this flow stops - the loop closes, per the standing rule that a created asset ends in the corrected asset, not in text. A GLM editor reads the card, the read and the audit and writes ONE concrete edit instruction: the full target end state (every bulb named with its derived glow state, the switch still open) plus the audit's specific failures as relighting demands - the yellow bulb glows dim but its switch is open: make it completely dark, clear unlit glass, no halo. The editor is gated in content, a rule this catalog learned the hard way from Molecule Check, whose flawless round-1 model was recoloured by a repair it never needed: on a perfect audit the editor must demand a verbatim unchanged copy and forbid anything being relit, darkened or moved. Muse Edit applies the notes to the round-1 picture. The corrected board is the flow's deliverable. A second blind reader re-inspects it, and the same auditor scores round 2 against the same card, so the fix is itself audited and the reader can see exactly what the edit bought - or cost. The Decide verdict stays, demoted to a side output on round 1.

Why this axis is new: the catalog's nearest neighbours are settle-check, balance-check and venn-check, and the differences are mechanical. In settle-check a sorting law derives a PERMUTATION - the vertical order of five bands; here a circuit law derives a STATE VECTOR - one glow state per bulb - from the TOPOLOGY of the wiring (connectivity and one switch position), and three different rules fire on three sub-circuits inside the same picture. In balance-check one equation (torque) decides one binary outcome (which side dips); here the audit also carries an EQUALITY axis with no analogue there: the series pair pass only if they match EACH OTHER, so a board where both are wrong in different ways fails twice over. In venn-check the logic is set membership drawn as overlap; nothing there conducts, and no element's appearance is a physical consequence of a connection drawn elsewhere in the picture. The characteristic failures are electrical, not spatial: a dead bulb lit because it is the largest, and a series pair glowing unevenly because the painter lit the nearer one more. Not the closed plan, render and recount vein: nothing is placed in a zone and there is nothing to count beyond four bulbs; a per-bulb physical consequence is derived from a wiring graph, produced, read blind and repaired.

Honest limits, in the open: the audit reads glow in three coarse states because finer brightness vocabulary turns the audit into naming arguments - a bulb at three-quarter brightness must be called BRIGHT or DIM by the reader, and the auditor scores the call it gets. Glow is judged against the board's own light (halo, pool on the wood), so a painter who floods the whole scene with ambient light can blur the DIM/OFF boundary; the reader instructions anchor OFF to clear unlit glass for that reason. The wiring itself is scored only through its visible switch and its consequences - the auditor does not trace individual wires in the render, so a board could in principle glow correctly over wrongly drawn wires and pass; the switch axis and the routing demanded in the prompt are the partial guards, stated here rather than hidden. The circuit law is the textbook school-lab version (identical bulbs, series bulbs share equally); real filaments are nonlinear, and the flow audits whether the painter honoured the carded law, not a bench measurement. One repair round is unrolled, not a loop - if round 2 still lights the dead bulb, the flow shows it rather than spending again.
